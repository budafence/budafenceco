Texas Storm Damage: Emergency Fence Repair & Insurance Tips

By Buda Fence Company  ·  7 min read

Texas weather is unforgiving. From the severe thunderstorms that roll through Austin and Buda in spring to the straight-line winds that hit Kyle and Dripping Springs in summer, your fence takes the brunt of every storm. When it comes down, here's exactly what to do — from immediate safety steps to filing your insurance claim and getting your property secured again fast.

Step 1: Safety First — Before You Touch Anything

Before assessing damage, check for downed power lines. If you see any, stay away and call your utility company immediately — do not approach until the line is confirmed de-energized. Also watch for unstable tree limbs, sharp debris, and sections of fence that may be under tension from fallen trees.

Step 2: Document Everything Before Any Cleanup

Before touching or moving anything, document the full extent of the damage with photos and video. Walk the entire fence line. Your insurance claim depends on this documentation — the more thorough the better. Capture wide shots showing the overall scope and close-ups of broken posts, damaged panels, and gate hardware. If you have "before" photos anywhere — from Google Street View, your phone's camera roll, or real estate listing photos — save those too.

Common Storm Damage Types in Central Texas

  • Leaning or fallen posts: Saturated clay soil loses holding strength fast. Posts — especially wood posts — can lean or topple under sustained wind load.
  • Broken or missing panels and pickets: Flying debris and direct wind force break boards, knock out sections, and can take out entire fence runs.
  • Warped or water-damaged wood: Prolonged saturation warps boards, accelerates rot, and compromises structural integrity.
  • Rust on metal fences: Hail chips protective coating, opening the metal to moisture and oxidation.
  • Damaged gates: Gates are the most vulnerable section — high winds misalign hinges, break latches, and can pull the gate frame entirely out of plumb.

Step 3: Secure Your Property While You Wait for Repairs

A major breach in your fence is a security and safety issue — especially if you have children or dogs. Temporary options while waiting for professional repairs:

  • Tarps or heavy plastic sheeting: Cover large gaps with tarps secured with zip ties for temporary privacy.
  • Temporary fencing panels: Available at equipment rental companies for larger breaches.
  • Brace leaning sections: Prop unstable sections with 2x4s or metal T-posts to prevent further collapse.

Step 4: File Your Insurance Claim

What Texas Policies Typically Cover

Most standard Texas homeowner's policies cover fence damage from wind and hail under "named perils." Flood damage almost always requires a separate flood insurance policy. Review your policy carefully — pay attention to your deductible, which is the amount you pay before coverage kicks in.

Filing Process

  1. Review your policy — confirm coverage, deductible, and the claim reporting window (most policies require prompt reporting).
  2. Submit your documentation — all photos, videos, and a written list of damaged components.
  3. Get contractor estimates — your insurance company will require at least one itemized estimate from a licensed contractor. We provide insurance-ready estimates for every storm damage call we respond to.
  4. File promptly — don't delay. Keep a log of every communication with your insurer including names, dates, and what was discussed.
  5. Negotiate if needed — you have the right to dispute a settlement offer. A public adjuster can represent you if the company's offer doesn't cover your actual repair costs.

Choosing a Contractor After a Storm

After major storms, unqualified "storm chasers" flood the area offering quick, cheap repairs. Protect yourself:

  • Verify licensing and insurance — any contractor working on your property should carry general liability and workers' comp insurance.
  • Check reviews — look at Google reviews and confirm the business has a real local presence, not just a temporary operation set up after the storm.
  • Get a written contract — scope of work, materials, total cost, and completion timeline in writing before any work begins.
  • Be wary of large upfront deposits — a standard deposit is 10–30%. Anyone asking for more than 50% upfront is a red flag.
  • Use a local contractor you can hold accountable — storm chasers leave town when the work dries up. Local contractors stand behind their work long-term.

How to Make Your Fence More Storm-Resistant

The #1 upgrade for storm resistance is switching from wood posts to galvanized steel PostMaster posts. Wood posts in saturated Central Texas clay lose holding strength rapidly — that's why entire fence runs collapse while the boards are still intact. Steel posts set in concrete hold firm in saturated soil and significantly reduce storm damage risk. Every fence we install uses steel posts for exactly this reason.

Regular inspections also matter. A post that's already slightly loose will fail in the first gust. Catching and fixing loose posts before storm season hits is far cheaper than replacing whole fence sections after.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do immediately after storm damage?

Check for safety hazards first — especially downed power lines. Then document everything with photos before touching anything. Secure your perimeter temporarily, then call a fence contractor for a professional assessment.

Will homeowner's insurance cover my fence damage?

Most Texas policies cover wind and hail damage. Flood damage requires a separate policy. Check your deductible — for minor damage, the cost may be below your deductible, making a cash repair more practical than filing a claim.

Should I repair or replace my storm-damaged fence?

If damage is localized to a few boards or one section, repair is the right call. If multiple posts failed or more than 20% of the fence is compromised, replacement is usually more cost-effective. We'll give you an honest assessment on the spot.

How can I protect my fence from future storms?

Use galvanized steel posts set in concrete — they hold far better than wood posts in saturated soil. Inspect your fence before storm season and fix any loose posts or boards. A fence in good structural condition will survive most storms that would knock down a neglected one.
